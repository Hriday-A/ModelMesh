"ovarian cancer is often called a silent killer because its early symptoms are subtle and easily attributed to other conditions. however, when the disease disseminates throughout the abdominal and pelvic cavities, a range of signs and symptoms emerge that reflect the extent of peritoneal spread, ascites formation, bowel involvement, and compression or invasion of pelvic structures.

one of the most common manifestations of abdominal and pelvic dissemination is ascites, the accumulation of fluid within the peritoneal cavity. patients frequently present with increasing abdominal girth, bloating, and a feeling of fullness. the abdomen may appear distended, and fluid shifts can be detected on physical examination as shifting dullness or a fluid wave. ascites results from peritoneal seeding, impaired lymphatic drainage, and increased vascular permeability caused by tumor implants on the peritoneal surfaces.

persistent abdominal pain and pelvic pain are hallmark symptoms. as tumor deposits implant on the parietal peritoneum, the omentum, and the bowel serosa, patients experience diffuse or localized discomfort, cramping, or a dull ache. the pain may worsen with movement, eating, or deep breathing when the diaphragmatic peritoneum is involved.

gastrointestinal symptoms are extremely common because disseminated disease often affects the intestines. these include early satiety, nausea, vomiting, indigestion, changes in bowel habits such as constipation or diarrhea, and sensations of incomplete evacuation. in advanced cases, tumor encasement of the bowel or malignant adhesions can cause partial or complete bowel obstruction, presenting with colicky abdominal pain, distension, obstipation, and vomiting. bowel obstruction is often a recurring problem in advanced ovarian cancer and may represent a surgical emergency.

urinary symptoms arise when pelvic tumor masses compress or invade the bladder or ureters. patients may report urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, difficulty emptying the bladder, or a sensation of pressure. ureteral compression can lead to hydronephrosis and, if bilateral, may impair renal function and cause flank pain or rising creatinine levels.

pelvic masses and pelvic pressure are frequently detected. patients may feel a lump or heaviness in the pelvis, and clinicians can often palpate a fixed, irregular, nodular mass on pelvic examination. a pelvic mass may also be discovered incidentally on imaging. because ovarian cancer spreads by direct extension and peritoneal seeding, the pouch of douglas, the rectouterine space, commonly harbors deposits, producing a feeling of rectal pressure or pain with defecation.

weight loss, cachexia, and loss of appetite frequently accompany disseminated disease. the cancer induces metabolic changes, cytokine release, and mechanical interference with eating, leading to significant unintentional weight loss despite the paradoxical increase in abdominal size from ascites and tumor burden.

fatigue and generalized weakness are common constitutional symptoms resulting from anemia, malnutrition, chronic inflammation, and the metabolic demands of widespread tumor. anemia may develop from chronic blood loss, bone marrow suppression, or cancer-related inflammation.

in some cases, patients present with symptoms of pleural effusion, such as shortness of breath or pleuritic chest pain, when malignant cells cross the diaphragm and fluid accumulates in the pleural space, a condition sometimes called meigs-like physiology in epithelial ovarian cancer. diaphragmatic spread can also cause hiccups or shoulder tip pain referred from diaphragmatic irritation.
